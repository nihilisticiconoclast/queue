Probability · queueing theory

Nobody arrives on schedule

A coffee kiosk serves one customer a minute, on average, and can make a drink in forty-eight seconds, on average. It ought to keep up easily. Instead it has four people at it on average, and a wait of over three minutes. The reason is in the two words ‘on average’, and two distributions account for nearly all of it: the Poisson, for how many people turn up, and the exponential, for how long until the next one. This page starts at the door, builds up to the queue, derives each formula and runs a simulation beside it so the two can be compared.

Counting arrivals

Suppose customers come through the door independently of one another, one at a time, at a steady average rate of \lambda per minute. Nobody coordinates, and the rate at ten past is the same as the rate at twenty past. How many arrive in a window of t minutes?

Chop the window into m slivers, each so short that it can hold at most one arrival. Each sliver then contains an arrival with probability \lambda t/m, independently of the others, so the count is binomial. Let the slivers shrink:

P(N(t)=k)=\binom{m}{k}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda t}{m}\Bigr)^{k}\Bigl(1-\frac{\lambda t}{m}\Bigr)^{m-k}\;\xrightarrow{\;m\to\infty\;}\;e^{-\lambda t}\,\frac{(\lambda t)^{k}}{k!}

That limit is the Poisson distribution with mean \lambda t. The binomial coefficient supplies the 1/k!, and the long run of empty slivers supplies the e^{-\lambda t}. Its variance is also \lambda t, which is the first thing to check in real data: if the counts per window vary much more than their mean, the arrivals are not as independent as assumed.

The gap to the next one

Turn the question round. Instead of counting arrivals in a fixed time, time the wait for one arrival. The gap T is longer than t exactly when the window of length t is empty, and the Poisson formula with k=0 gives that at once:

P(T>t)=P(N(t)=0)=e^{-\lambda t},\qquad f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t},\qquad \mathbb{E}[T]=\frac{1}{\lambda}

This is the exponential distribution. Its mean and its standard deviation are both 1/\lambda, its median is the shorter \ln 2/\lambda, and its most likely value is zero: short gaps are the commonest kind, which is where the clumps come from.

It does not remember

Suppose you have already waited s minutes and nobody has come. How much longer?

P(T>s+t\mid T>s)=\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}=e^{-\lambda t}

Exactly as long as if you had just started. The exponential is the only continuous distribution with this property, and it is what makes the later formulas tractable: the future of the queue depends on how many people are in it, and not on how long any of them has been there. The bus-stop paradox

If buses come at random every ten minutes on average and you turn up at a random moment, the lack of memory says your expected wait is the full ten minutes, not five. That looks wrong, since you must arrive partway through a gap. The resolution is that you are more likely to land in a long gap than a short one, in proportion to its length, so the gap you land in averages 2/\lambda, twice the typical one, and half of that is 1/\lambda. The figures under the sliders measure both from the gaps seen so far. The same effect is why the queue you find on arrival tends to be longer than the average queue the staff would report.

Waiting for the nth customer

The time until the nth arrival, S_n, is the sum of n independent exponential gaps. Its mean and variance simply add up, and its distribution is called the Erlang (a gamma distribution with a whole-number shape):

\mathbb{E}[S_n]=\frac{n}{\lambda},\qquad \operatorname{sd}(S_n)=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\lambda},\qquad f_n(t)=\frac{\lambda^{n}t^{\,n-1}e^{-\lambda t}}{(n-1)!}

To find the chance that the nth customer has arrived by time t there is no need to integrate that density. The nth arrival has happened by t exactly when at least n arrivals have been counted by t, so the answer is a Poisson tail:

P(S_n\le t)=P(N(t)\ge n)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-\lambda t}\,\frac{(\lambda t)^{k}}{k!}

The two charts show the two sides of that identity. On the left, the shaded area is the part of the waiting-time curve before t. On the right, the filled bars are the counts of n or more. The two shaded amounts are always equal. Drag across the left chart to move t.

Two things are worth seeing here. With n=1 the curve is the exponential again, piled against zero. As n grows it moves right and turns into a bell, because a sum of many independent gaps is approximately normal, with mean n/\lambda and standard deviation \sqrt{n}/\lambda. And the relative spread, 1/\sqrt{n}, shrinks: the tenth customer’s arrival time is uncertain by about a third of its mean, the hundredth’s by a tenth.

Merging, splitting and racing

Three facts about Poisson streams do most of the work in practice, and all three come from the sliver argument.

Merging. Two independent Poisson streams, at rates \lambda_1 and \lambda_2, combine into a single Poisson stream at rate \lambda_1+\lambda_2. Customers from the platform and from the street make one stream at the kiosk.

Splitting. If each arrival is independently of one kind with probability p, the arrivals of that kind form a Poisson stream at rate p\lambda, the rest form one at rate (1-p)\lambda, and the two are independent of each other. Knowing how many coffees were ordered in the last hour tells you nothing about how many teas.

Racing. If two exponential clocks run side by side at rates \mu_A and \mu_B, the first to ring does so after an exponential time at rate \mu_A+\mu_B, and it is clock A with probability \mu_A/(\mu_A+\mu_B). This is what governs two baristas working at once, or the contest between the next arrival and the next departure.

One more fact is useful for checking data. Given that exactly n customers arrived in a window, their arrival times are scattered over it uniformly and independently, as if each had picked a moment with a pin. If the arrivals in your data bunch towards one end of the hour, the rate was not constant over that hour.

One server, and the line behind

Now put a counter behind the door. Customers arrive in a Poisson stream at rate \lambda. One server takes an exponential time over each, at rate \mu per minute, so a mean of 1/\mu minutes. Anyone who finds the server busy waits in line, first come first served. This is the M/M/1 queue. The single number that matters most is the traffic intensity, the fraction of time the server has to be working:

\rho=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}

If \rho\ge 1 work arrives faster than it can be done and the line grows without limit. If \rho<1 the queue settles into a statistical equilibrium, and finding it takes one observation. In the long run the number of times the system steps up from n customers to n+1 must equal the number of times it steps back down. Steps up happen at rate \lambda while there are n present, steps down at rate \mu while there are n+1, so with p_n the fraction of time spent with n in the system,

\lambda\,p_n=\mu\,p_{n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_n=(1-\rho)\,\rho^{\,n}

a geometric distribution. Everything else follows from it. The server is idle a fraction p_0=1-\rho of the time. The mean number in the system is the mean of that geometric distribution, and the mean time each customer spends comes from Little’s law, L=\lambda W, which holds for any queue at all: the people present as you leave are the ones who arrived during your stay.

L=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho},\qquad W=\frac{L}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda},\qquad W_q=W-\frac{1}{\mu}=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda},\qquad L_q=\lambda W_q=\frac{\rho^{2}}{1-\rho}

Here L and W count the customer being served, and L_q and W_q count only the line. The whole distribution of the wait is available too: a customer’s total time in the system is itself exponential, at rate \mu-\lambda, and the chance of queueing for longer than t is

P(W_q>t)=\rho\,e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t}

That slowness is a property of queues and not of the simulation: congestion comes in long episodes, so a busy system has to be watched for a long time before its averages mean anything. An hour’s observation of a real counter at \rho=0.9 tells you very little.

Set \lambda above \mu and the formula column gives up. There is no equilibrium, and the line grows at about \lambda-\mu customers a minute for as long as you let it.

More tills

With c identical servers fed from one line, the M/M/c queue, the same balancing argument applies. Steps up still happen at rate \lambda, but steps down happen at n\mu while n servers are busy, and at c\mu once all of them are. Write a=\lambda/\mu for the offered load, the number of servers the work would occupy if it arrived evenly, and \rho=a/c for the share of each server’s time that is taken. The chance that an arriving customer finds every server busy and has to wait is Erlang’s C formula:

C(c,a)=\frac{\dfrac{a^{c}}{c!}\,\dfrac{1}{1-\rho}}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{c-1}\frac{a^{k}}{k!}+\frac{a^{c}}{c!}\,\frac{1}{1-\rho}}

Once everyone is busy the system empties at rate c\mu and behaves like a single fast server, which gives the rest:

W_q=\frac{C(c,a)}{c\mu-\lambda},\qquad P(W_q>t)=C(c,a)\,e^{-(c\mu-\lambda)t},\qquad L_q=\lambda W_q

With c=1 these reduce to the formulas of the last section. The counter above already understands them: raise its c slider and both of its columns follow.

Two things in the table are general. First, the step from just enough servers to one more is very large and the steps after it are small, so the right number is usually one or two above the bare minimum a and rarely more. Second, look at the last column. Splitting the same staff into separate lines, each with its own share of the arrivals, always produces a longer wait than a single line feeding all of them, because a separate line lets one server stand idle while customers wait at another. That is the argument for the single snaking queue at a post office, and for pooling anything that can be pooled.

The knee, and what variability costs

Every formula so far has 1-\rho on the bottom. That is the most important practical fact about queues: waiting does not rise in proportion to how busy the server is. It is nearly flat up to moderate loads, bends, and then climbs steeply. Going from 80% busy to 90% doubles the wait and more; from 90% to 95% doubles it again.

The other ingredient is how variable the service is. The exponential assumption can be dropped for a single server. If service times have mean \mathbb{E}[S] and coefficient of variation c_s (standard deviation divided by mean), the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula gives the mean wait in line for any service distribution whatever:

W_q=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\cdot\frac{1+c_s^{2}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[S]

The exponential has c_s=1, which makes the middle factor 1 and returns the M/M/1 result. A machine that takes exactly the same time over every job has c_s=0, and halves the wait. Service that is mostly quick with the occasional very long job has c_s above 1, and the wait grows with its square. Only the mean and variance of the service time enter; the shape beyond that does not matter to the mean wait.

The ring is a simulation of 200,000 customers at the chosen settings. Press Simulate again a few times at \rho=0.5 and it barely moves. Do the same at \rho=0.95 and it jumps about, for the reason given earlier: near saturation even 200,000 customers is a short sample.

Variability in the arrivals matters in the same way. For a single server with arrivals that are not Poisson, Kingman’s approximation replaces the middle factor with (c_a^{2}+c_s^{2})/2, where c_a is the coefficient of variation of the gaps between arrivals. Appointments, which make arrivals regular, shorten queues for exactly this reason.

A ready reckoner

The standard questions, in one place. Enter the figures for a queue and every answer is worked from the formulas above. Times are in minutes and rates are per minute, but any unit will do so long as it is the same throughout.

Reading the page

The numbers

\lambda is the arrival rate in customers per minute and \mu is the rate at which one busy server completes services, so 1/\lambda is the mean gap between arrivals and 1/\mu the mean service time. c is the number of servers, a=\lambda/\mu the offered load and \rho=a/c the utilisation, the long-run fraction of each server’s time spent serving. N(t) is the number of arrivals in a window of length t, T a single gap, and S_n the time of the nth arrival. L is the mean number of customers in the system and W the mean time one spends there, both including service; L_q and W_q are the same for the line alone. Wherever a readout shows a large figure with a smaller one beneath it, the large one is measured from the simulation and the small one is the formula. In the first panel None at all is the share of windows with no arrivals, to be compared with e^{-\lambda t}. In the gaps panel Longer than s is the share of gaps that outlasted s, and Mean wait after s is the average further wait among those. In the counter’s table, averages of numbers present are taken over time and averages of waits are taken over customers; Had to wait is the share of customers who found every server busy. In the variability panel waits are in multiples of the mean service time, so they can be read for any service speed.

The controls

At the door, Play runs time forward, the two sliders set the arrival rate and the window length, +1,000 windows skips ahead without drawing, and Reset clears everything seen. Changing either slider also clears it, since counts taken at one rate cannot be pooled with counts at another. The gaps panel reads the same stream, so its rate slider is the same control; s changes nothing about the stream, only which gaps are picked out. In the nth arrival the three sliders, or a drag on the left chart, set the formula’s inputs directly; nothing there is simulated. In splitting, New half hour draws a fresh thirty minutes; in racing the sliders set the two rates and 20,000 races are run each time they move. At the counter, the sliders set arrivals, service rate per server and number of servers; Speed changes only the pace; changing any rate keeps the customers who are present and restarts the averages; Empty and restart clears the room as well. The staffing and reckoner fields are plain inputs to the formulas. In variability, the sliders or a drag on the chart set the load, and Simulate again repeats the 200,000-customer run with fresh random numbers.

What is idealised

The arrival rate is constant. Real rates rise and fall through the day, and the formulas then apply only within a period short enough for the rate to be steady and long enough for the queue to settle, which at high loads may be no period at all. Customers here are infinitely patient: nobody is put off by the length of the line, nobody gives up, and the room never fills. Service is first come, first served, by identical servers, with service times independent of each other and of the queue. The exponential service time, with its most likely value at zero, is a poor description of most real services, which is why the variability section matters; but the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula covers one server only, and with several servers and general service times there is no exact formula at all. Every equilibrium figure is a long-run average, and says nothing about the first half hour after opening. The simulations use a seeded pseudo-random generator, so a given sequence of button presses replays identically. The variability simulation draws service times from a gamma distribution with the chosen coefficient of variation, discards the first 5,000 customers and averages the rest. The counter’s chart of states stops at 40; time spent above that is added to the last bar.
